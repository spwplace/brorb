

Brorb is envisioned as a "breathing orb" – a device or simulation that breathes with the user by expanding and contracting in sync with their respiration. Unlike a simple linear (“lerp”) animation, Brorb will be driven by an authentic physiological model of human breathing, capturing the neurophysiology of respiratory control. The default breathing rate is set to a slow 4 breaths per minute (15-second cycles) to promote relaxation. The system will also incorporate real-time biofeedback from the user: it listens to breathing sounds via a microphone and monitors heart rate via a photoplethysmography (PPG) sensor, using these inputs to adjust and personalize the orb’s behavior. With no hard performance constraints (the target platform is a MacBook Pro), we can afford a detailed simulation. While Rust was initially considered for its performance, rapid development in Python is likely more practical for prototyping this complex model, with the option to optimize or port components to Rust later if needed.

Key design requirements include:

    Multilevel Physiological Modeling: Simulate the breathing process at multiple levels – from neural rhythm generation in the brainstem, to lung mechanics and airflow, and even autonomic interactions (heart rate changes). The goal is a mechanistically valid model that captures the nuances of real respiration.

    Real-Time Sensor Integration: Continuously use microphone input to detect the user’s inhale/exhale events, and PPG input for heart rate and heart rate variability. These signals will feed into the model so the orb can truly “breathe with you,” responding to your breaths and physiological state.

    High Fidelity & No Performance Constraints: Aim for an “autistically detailed” simulation incorporating known physiology (neural circuits, feedback loops, etc.). Since this will run on a modern laptop without strict resource limits, we can employ computationally intensive models if needed.

    Development in Python (Rust as Future Option): Favor Python for initial implementation due to its rich scientific libraries and faster iteration. Rust remains an aspiration for a future, high-performance implementation once the model is validated.

Neurophysiology of Breathing Control

At the core of the simulation is the respiratory central pattern generator (CPG) in the brainstem. In mammals, rhythmic breathing is generated by specialized neural circuits located in the medulla and pons of the brainstem. These circuits autonomously produce the oscillating neural activity that underlies the inhale-exhale cycle. Notably, the normal respiratory rhythm consists of three phases of neural activity – inspiration, post-inspiration (the early part of expiration), and late expiration. This three-phase pattern is produced by an intricate neural network of interconnected excitatory and inhibitory neurons in specific regions: primarily the pre-Bötzinger complex (pre-BötC) and Bötzinger complex (BötC) in the ventrolateral medulla.

    Pre-Bötzinger Complex: This region contains intrinsically rhythmogenic inspiratory neurons. These neurons have special ionic currents (e.g. a persistent sodium current I<sub>NaP</sub>) that enable them to generate bursting activity. In essence, they act as pacemakers driving inhalation. The excitatory bursts from pre-BötC neurons initiate each inspiration and drive the phrenic nerve and respiratory muscles. The simulation will include a modeled inspiratory drive, e.g. an oscillator representing the aggregate activity of pre-BötC pacemaker neurons. The inclusion of a persistent sodium current or an equivalent mechanism in the model is important to produce realistic burst dynamics, as established in computational models of respiratory rhythm generation.

    Bötzinger Complex: This adjacent region contains populations of inhibitory neurons that are active during expiration (including so-called post-inspiratory and augmenting-expiratory neurons). These inhibitory neurons project to the inspiratory group and each other, creating a reciprocal inhibition architecture. During inspiration, pre-BötC activity suppresses expiratory neurons; then as inspiration ends, the BötC inhibitory neurons strongly inhibit the inspiratory neurons, allowing expiration to occur. This reciprocal inhibition between inspiratory and expiratory populations produces a robust oscillatory cycle with distinct phases. The model will implement this via mutually inhibitory units or equations to simulate the phase-switching: when the inspiratory drive phase ends, the inhibitory drive grows (simulating the post-I and expiratory phase), and vice versa, reproducing the natural inhale-exhale rhythm.

    Three-Phase Rhythm: Through the above interactions, the core CPG network inherently generates the rhythmic pattern of inspiration → post-inspiration → expiration. The excitatory pre-BötC circuit provides the drive for inspiration, while the inhibitory circuits in BötC (and early-inspiratory interneurons in pre-BötC) coordinate the timing of phase transitions. This will allow Brorb to have a realistic breathing waveform rather than a simple sine wave. For example, human inspiration typically features a smooth ramp-up of diaphragm activity and a shorter active period than the passive expiration – the model can capture this asymmetry. We will calibrate the model’s intrinsic rhythm to ~0.067 Hz (4 breaths/min), but it should be capable of speeding up or slowing down in response to inputs (like CO₂ levels or conscious override), as real CPGs do.

    Computational Model Approach: To implement the CPG in code, we have options. Detailed spiking neuron models (Hodgkin-Huxley type) have been used in research to reproduce respiratory neuron firing patterns, but these can be computationally heavy. Fortunately, researchers have also developed simplified “activity-based” models that represent each neural population with a small set of ordinary differential equations, rather than simulating thousands of spikes. These reduced models capture the essential behavior (phase oscillations, responses to perturbations) with far fewer parameters. For Brorb, a reasonable strategy is to adopt such a simplified model – for instance, using a set of coupled nonlinear ODEs representing the activity of the inspiratory (pre-BötC) and expiratory (BötC) neuron populations. This keeps it mechanistic (grounded in known neural circuitry) but tractable to simulate in real time. We will ensure the model can exhibit phenomena observed in real physiology, such as changes in breathing rate or amplitude if certain inputs are altered (e.g. simulating drug effects on I<sub>NaP</sub> or changing inhibitory strength, as in published models). In summary, the neurocomputational core of Brorb will be a biologically inspired oscillator rather than an arbitrary function – giving our orb a lifelike breathing pattern generated from first principles of neurophysiology.

Lung Mechanics and Physiological Feedback

While the brainstem CPG generates the neural rhythm, the mechanical side of breathing deals with how air moves in and out of lungs, which we will simulate for the orb’s physical “breathing” motion. The output of the CPG is neural signals (e.g. via the phrenic nerve) that cause the diaphragm and other respiratory muscles to contract. In our model, we can represent this as an inspiratory “motor drive” signal coming from the CPG module. This drive creates a negative pressure in the lungs (or an equivalent expansion force) that causes lung volume to increase (inhalation). When the drive ceases, the lungs and chest recoil passively, leading to exhalation.

We will implement a simplified lung mechanics model to convert neural drive into volume changes. A common approach is to treat the lung-thorax system as a compliant compartment with airflow resistance. For example, a two-element model uses an equation:

PT(t)=R⋅Q(t)+V(t)C+PEEP,PT​(t)=R⋅Q(t)+CV(t)​+PEEP​,

where P<sub>T</sub> is transpulmonary pressure (muscle-generated pressure), Q = dV/dt is airflow, V is lung volume, R is airway resistance, C is lung compliance, and PEEP is the end-expiratory pressure baseline. In simulation, given the CPG’s output we can compute a driving pressure pattern (active during inspiration) and solve this equation (an ODE) to obtain the changing lung volume over time. This will determine how the orb expands/contracts. For instance, during the inspiratory phase the model will apply a pressure forcing that increases volume; as volume rises, elastic recoil (V/C term) and resistance (R·Q term) resist the expansion, naturally slowing and capping the volume – yielding a realistic inhalation curve rather than a simple linear rise. During expiration, the drive is off, so pressure drops and the volume returns toward the functional residual capacity (the PEEP can represent a baseline lung volume). By adjusting parameters (compliance, resistance), we can mimic different breathing characteristics (e.g. a stiff lung vs. a flexible one, fast vs. slow filling). Brorb’s orb animation will likely map the orb’s radius or glow to the simulated lung volume in real time.

Mechanoreceptor Feedback: A key aspect of physiologic breathing control is feedback from the lungs to the brain. Stretch receptors in the lungs sense inflation – if you inhale deeply, they send signals via the vagus nerve to the brainstem to inhibit further inspiration. This is the classic Hering–Breuer inflation reflex, which prevents over-inflation. Our model will include a similar feedback: as the simulated lung volume increases, it will trigger an inhibitory input to the inspiratory neurons, helping to terminate the breath at an appropriate point. This creates a closed-loop control: the brain drives the lungs, and lung feedback in turn moderates the brain’s activity. In practical terms, we can implement this as a negative feedback term where if modelled tidal volume exceeds a certain threshold, it suppresses the pre-BötC drive activity, ending the inspiratory phase. This will make the simulated breathing self-regulating and more robust – for example, if for some reason the neural drive were stronger or prolonged in one breath, the reflex would kick in and ensure the breath ends, just as in biological systems. Conversely, if lung volume is low (or if an exhale is prolonged), reduced stretch feedback can allow the next inspiration to initiate. Including this mechanoreceptor loop aligns with the user’s request for mechanistic validity “and more,” adding realism beyond a simple open-loop oscillator.

Chemoreceptor Feedback: The ultimate purpose of breathing is to maintain blood gas homeostasis (O₂ and CO₂ levels). Chemoreceptors monitor these levels – central chemoreceptors in the medulla respond to CO₂/pH, and peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid bodies respond primarily to O₂. When CO₂ rises or O₂ falls, these sensors stimulate the respiratory center to breathe faster or deeper. In an “autistically detailed” simulation, we can incorporate a simple model of CO₂ accumulation and clearance. For example, we could have a variable for CO₂ level that gradually increases when breathing is too slow/shallow (because CO₂ production continues in the body model) and decreases when breathing is fast enough. This CO₂ level can feed into the CPG model as a drive that modulates the breathing rate and amplitude (analogous to the real chemoreflex). If the user or orb stays at 4 breaths/min, CO₂ might accumulate; the model could then slightly increase respiratory drive to compensate, achieving a new steady state. Implementing full gas exchange physics might be beyond scope, but a qualitative feedback loop can be included. The key is that the breathing orb will not just run on a fixed clock – it will have internal state (like “CO₂”) that influences its rhythm, making it adapt to conditions. This echoes how real breathing is regulated by blood gas levels.

In summary, the mechanical and feedback layer will ensure that Brorb’s breathing is realistically self-regulating. The combination of a neural rhythm generator, lung mechanics, and sensor feedback loops (stretch and chemical) forms an integrated control system much like that of a real human: the brainstem CPG provides baseline rhythm, the lungs and chemoreceptors provide feedback to adjust depth and rate, and everything works in concert to maintain stable breathing. This multi-level simulation (neural → mechanical → feedback) addresses the “three levels and more” requested, providing a comprehensive respiration model.
Autonomic Integration: Heart Rate and Breathing

An important extension of the breathing model is its interaction with the autonomic nervous system, particularly the heart. In normal physiology, breathing and heart rate are closely coupled by a phenomenon known as respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA). RSA refers to the cyclical variation in heart rate that occurs at the breathing frequency: specifically, heart rate speeds up during inhalation (shorter R-R intervals) and slows down during exhalation (longer intervals). This is a benign, even beneficial, arrhythmia reflecting vagus nerve activity – essentially, during inspiration the parasympathetic (vagal) tone to the heart is transiently suppressed (allowing heart rate to rise), and during expiration vagal tone is restored (slowing the heart). One purpose of RSA is thought to be improving gas exchange efficiency by matching heartbeats to lung ventilation cycles.

For our purposes, RSA is a convenient measurable outcome of mind-body connection: a higher RSA (i.e., a bigger difference between inhale heart rate and exhale heart rate) generally indicates a calm state with strong vagal tone, whereas reduced RSA may indicate stress or reduced parasympathetic activity. Brorb will utilize heart rate data from the PPG to monitor RSA and overall autonomic state. By analyzing the subtle beat-to-beat changes in the PPG signal in relation to the breathing cycle, the system can determine if the user’s heart rhythm is synchronizing with their breaths. This gives us a window into the user’s relaxation level. In fact, slow, deep breathing around 4–6 breaths per minute is known to maximize RSA and has been used in biofeedback training to increase vagal tone. Research confirms that breathing at ~4 breaths/min can improve measures of calm and even reduce stress hormone (cortisol) levels. We therefore expect that guiding the user to this slow rate (the orb’s default) and having them synchronize with it will enhance their physiological coherence (heart-brain synchronization) and relaxation.

From a design standpoint, we can incorporate the heart interaction in a few ways. One-way coupling (feed-forward): The simplest is to have the breathing simulation influence a subtle aspect of the orb related to heart. For example, as the orb “inhales” and “exhales” with the user, we could make the orb’s light or color gently pulsate at the user’s heartbeat in real time. The PPG gives us a continuous heart rate; we could translate that into a minor oscillation or a blip on the orb (like a faint heartbeat glow superimposed on the breathing glow). This would create a powerful biofeedback loop: the user literally sees their heart and lungs in rhythm. Two-way integration (feedback): We can also have the heart data influence the breathing model. For instance, if the user’s heart rate is very high (indicative of stress or exercise), the orb might automatically adjust the breathing rate upward slightly (since in exercise, breathing rate increases) or change color to signal “alert/high stress.” Conversely, as the user calms and heart rate drops, the orb could slow or dim accordingly. Another angle is using heart rate variability: if the orb detects low RSA (flat heart rate across breathing), it might prompt the user to take slower, deeper breaths to engage more vagal activity. This adaptive behavior would mimic how our bodies operate: under stress (sympathetic activation) we breathe faster/shallow, and under rest (parasympathetic) we breathe slower/deeper with high RSA – the orb can attempt to guide the user toward the latter state.

Importantly, the real-time visualization of stress or calm via the orb can be very impactful. For example, there is a biofeedback device (the Reflect Orb) that uses an LED light ring changing color to indicate the user’s stress level in real time. We can take inspiration from this: Brorb might glow blue or green when the user is calm (high HRV, coherent slow breathing) and perhaps yellow or red when the user is tense (elevated heart rate, irregular breathing). This immediate feedback makes the user aware of their internal state and encourages self-regulation. Since Brorb “breathes” with the user, the combination of synchronized breathing motion and responsive color feedback could powerfully reinforce relaxation: the user can actively see and feel that when they breathe slowly and steadily, the orb’s breathing matches theirs and the orb’s color shifts to a calming hue (rewarding the behavior). This merges the breathing simulation with biofeedback training, leveraging the heart-breath connection.

In the simulation, implementing RSA simply means recognizing that the heart rate isn’t constant but varies with breathing. We will process the PPG signal (which provides a pulse waveform or at least inter-beat intervals) and extract the instantaneous heart rate. By computing the heart rate as a function of the breathing phase (e.g. averaging heart rate during inhale vs exhale), we can quantify RSA. Though our orb model won’t necessarily cause RSA (that’s a property of the user’s physiology), it will track and display it. We could optionally include a simple model of how breathing could alter heart rate – for instance, increasing heart rate by a few BPM during inhalation due to reduced vagal drive. This could be done by a small function that modulates a “simulated user heart rate” in sync with the breathing model (if we ever run a fully closed simulation without a real user). However, when a real user is involved, it’s more straightforward to just use their PPG data as input. In summary, integrating the heart signals addresses the interaction between the respiratory and autonomic systems, adding another level of mechanistic and experiential depth to Brorb.
Breathing and Heart Rate Sensing

To make the orb truly interactive, we need robust methods to sense the user’s breathing and heart rate in real time. Here’s how we plan to achieve that:

    Breath Detection via Microphone: We will use the microphone to pick up the sound of the user’s breathing. Breathing sounds (especially exhalations through the nose or mouth) can be captured if the mic is reasonably close to the user. The raw audio will be analyzed to detect the timing of breaths. A simple approach is to monitor the audio amplitude or envelope in the frequency range of breath noise. When a person inhales, there might be a quieter sound (unless through mouth), and when exhaling, a more audible turbulent airflow sound. We can apply a band-pass filter to isolate typical breath sound frequencies and then use an adaptive threshold on the signal envelope to detect breaths. For example, one algorithm first finds the predominant frequency of the breathing sound and uses that to dynamically threshold the audio envelope, identifying each exhalation phase. We will draw from such methods: essentially, the software will mark the onset and offset of each breath (or at least each exhale, which is usually easier to hear) from the audio. In research tests, smartphone audio has been able to measure respiratory rate to within 1 breath per minute of accuracy by analyzing these sound patterns. For our purposes, exact precision is less critical than real-time responsiveness – if there’s a slight delay of say 100 ms in detecting the breath, that’s fine. The detected breathing events will be fed into the simulation: e.g., when an inhale is detected to start, we could synchronize the orb’s inspiratory phase with the user’s, and when an exhale is detected, we ensure the orb transitions to exhalation. Essentially the mic provides a timing signal to phase-lock the model’s rhythm to the user. We will also account for ambient noise (maybe require the environment to be quiet or use noise cancellation techniques) – robust detection might involve ignoring sudden noises that don’t fit the breathing pattern. If needed, machine learning classifiers (trained to recognize inhale vs exhale sounds) could be employed, but initially a simpler signal processing approach should suffice.

    Heart Rate from PPG: The PPG sensor (e.g. a finger or ear pulse sensor, or a smartwatch) provides a photoplethysmogram – essentially a waveform corresponding to blood volume changes with each heartbeat. From this, calculating heart rate is straightforward: measure the interval between pulses (peaks of the PPG waveform) to get instantaneous heart rate, or count pulses per minute. Many consumer devices do this automatically. In our system, we might interface with a wearable or a finger-clip sensor to stream the PPG data. The software will run a peak detection on the PPG signal to get each beat time. Then we can compute heart rate (and its variation) continuously. Because we want to tie it to breathing, we will likely align the heartbeats with the respiratory phase. One way is to time-stamp each heartbeat relative to the last detected breath start; over several breaths we can derive how the heart rate is changing with phase. If using a high-frequency PPG (many samples per second), we might even directly observe respiratory modulation in the waveform amplitude or baseline – indeed, some wearables estimate respiration rate from PPG by tracking subtle oscillations caused by breathing. (When you inhale, venous return to the heart changes and the PPG amplitude can slightly decrease, etc.) So, as a cross-check, we have two independent ways to get breathing: microphone and PPG-derived respiratory signal. If the microphone fails (e.g. user is silent and sensor is noisy), the PPG could act as a backup to estimate breathing rate, since many devices now offer that feature.

    Data Fusion and Timing: The microphone and PPG data will be handled in real time. Likely, the mic gives a more immediate indication of breath events (with only milliseconds of audio processing delay), whereas heart rate has an inherent delay (you typically compute HR over a moving window or from the last beat – so you know your heart rate “now” based on a beat that just occurred). We will run these sensing processes in parallel with the simulation. For example, the software might have one thread or asynchronous task listening to the microphone, detecting breaths, and another reading the PPG. Each time a breath is detected, it can send a trigger to the breathing model (e.g., “user started inhaling now”). The model can then adjust phase or possibly reset its cycle to match. If the user’s breathing is slower or faster than the orb’s default, the orb will adapt – effectively entraining to the user rather than forcing the user to follow it. Conversely, if the user is very out of sync or not breathing (holding breath), the orb could continue at a baseline 4/min and perhaps encourage the user to come back to that rhythm. The PPG heart rate will be slightly smoother data; we might update the orb’s color or biofeedback indicators every second or so based on heart rate trends, rather than trying to reflect each heartbeat in real time (though as mentioned a subtle heartbeat glow is possible, it might be too fast to visually track at ~60+ per minute). More likely, we use the heartbeat info to compute stress metrics like HRV (high-frequency HRV is basically RSA) and display those slowly. For instance, if the user’s HRV increases over a session, the orb’s color could shift toward green, signaling relaxation success.

    Accuracy and Calibration: We should note that breath detection from audio might need calibration for each user (microphone sensitivity, etc.). We may include a calibration where the user takes a few breaths and the system “learns” the sound pattern and adjusts threshold. Similarly, PPG sensors can sometimes pick up motion artifacts; if the user moves a lot, the HR reading could spike erroneously. Our design assumes the user will be relatively still, perhaps sitting and holding the orb or sitting near it. Since this is for breathing exercises/meditation, that’s a reasonable assumption. Nonetheless, the software will have to filter out noise – e.g., ignore breath detections that are too close together to be physiologically real, or smooth the heart rate signal to avoid sudden jumps that are not plausible.

By combining multi-modal sensing (audio + PPG), Brorb will have a robust perception of the user’s physiological signals. This data will make the simulation truly interactive and personalized: the orb is not just executing a pre-set breathing animation, it is literally linked to the user’s own breaths and heartbeat, creating a biofeedback loop. This satisfies the requirement to use microphone and PPG inputs, and ensures the final experience is grounded in the user’s real-time state.
Implementation Considerations (Python vs. Rust)

Developing Brorb’s simulation and real-time interactivity will involve a fair amount of numerical computation and signal processing. We have flexibility in choosing tools since performance is not the primary bottleneck (MacBook Pro hardware is quite capable). The two candidate languages are Rust and Python, each with pros and cons:

    Python (Preferred for Prototyping): Python offers an extensive ecosystem for scientific computing and prototyping. Libraries like NumPy/SciPy will allow us to integrate differential equations for the respiratory model efficiently (using vectorized operations or ODE solvers) without writing all the low-level code ourselves. We can also leverage libraries for signal processing – for example, librosa or PyAudio for audio capture and analysis, and perhaps heartpy or NeuroKit2 for PPG processing. In fact, tools like NeuroKit2 even have functions to simulate or analyze respiration and heart rate signals, which could speed up development. Python’s high-level nature means we can iterate quickly on the model: tweak parameters, try new feedback mechanisms, etc., with immediate results. This is important given the complexity – we’ll likely need to fine-tune the simulation to feel “right” for users. Another advantage is ease of integration: Python can interface with hardware or sensor APIs (Bluetooth LE for wearables, microphone drivers, etc.) with existing libraries. The downside of Python is runtime performance – a pure Python loop running a millisecond-by-millisecond neural simulation could be slow. However, we can mitigate this by using NumPy (which runs in C under the hood) for vectorized computations, or using just-in-time compilers like Numba if needed. Considering our model might only involve on the order of tens of state variables (not millions), Python should handle it in real time. For example, integrating a system of, say, 10 ODEs at 1 kHz sample rate is trivial for modern Python on a CPU. We might only need to be careful if we attempted a large spiking neural network simulation – but as discussed, we’ll likely use a reduced model. Python also allows interactive visualization (for debugging, we could plot the breathing waveforms, etc., in real time).

    Rust (Aspirational for Optimization): Rust is a systems programming language that offers C/C++ level performance with memory safety guarantees. Writing the simulation in Rust could yield a highly efficient executable, which might matter if we later deploy Brorb on a smaller device or incorporate much more detail (e.g., hundreds of neurons, or 3D graphics for the orb). Rust also excels at concurrency. We could have separate threads for sensor input, simulation, and rendering, without the Global Interpreter Lock that Python has. This means the microphone processing, PPG processing, and core simulation loop could run truly in parallel, yielding smoother real-time performance. Rust’s strong type system might help us avoid runtime errors in a complex simulation. That said, Rust lacks the breadth of ready-made scientific libraries that Python has. There are crates for numerical integration and signal processing (e.g., ndarray for numerics, rodio or cpal for audio, etc.), but the developer effort to assemble everything will be higher. Given that we are in a rapid development phase and the project is quite exploratory (figuring out the model details, tuning feedback loops), the rigidity of Rust might slow down experimentation. A practical path is to prototype in Python, get the algorithms working and validated, and then if needed, reimplement performance-critical parts in Rust or use Rust for the final product. We could even do a hybrid: Python for high-level orchestration and call into Rust for the simulation calculations via FFI or a library, if we find Python’s speed inadequate.

    Real-Time and GUI: Another consideration is the user interface or visualization of the orb. If we need to render a smooth animation of a breathing orb (say, a pulsating circle on screen or a physical device’s LED), we have to update it ~60 frames per second. Python can handle simple graphics (e.g., using Pygame or OpenGL bindings), but Rust could integrate with graphics engines or cross-platform UI more performantly (for example, a Rust app with a wgpu or OpenGL renderer for the orb visualization). However, since design agent might handle the actual visual design, we assume we just need to output the breathing state (like a radius or color value) to whatever interface. Python could do that by sending data to a front-end (like via websockets to a web UI, or a simple Tkinter GUI). Rust could create a standalone app. These are engineering details, but worth noting: if the orb becomes a product, a Rust implementation might be more suitable for distribution (a compiled binary) whereas Python might be easier during development and testing.

In conclusion, we plan to use Python initially due to its flexibility and rich ecosystem, ensuring we meet the project’s functionality goals first. We will keep the code modular so that performance-intensive components (like the ODE solver for the CPG model) can be swapped out or optimized later. If profiling shows any bottlenecks, we can optimize (perhaps by moving inner loops to NumPy or using Cython). Once the design is solid and if there is interest in a production version, we can consider translating the core simulation to Rust. This would give us a fast, low-level implementation that could run on various platforms (even potentially on embedded hardware if the orb became a standalone device). Rust’s strength in embedded systems could be relevant if, for instance, we ever put this breathing orb logic on a microcontroller inside a physical orb – a scenario where Python can’t run but Rust can. Thus, Rust remains an aspiration for the final form, aligning with the initial idea, but Python is the pragmatic choice to achieve a working, mechanistically accurate prototype of Brorb in the near term.
Conclusion and Next Steps

In performing this research, we have outlined a comprehensive plan for a breathing orb that is rooted in real physiology. We covered the neural underpinnings of breathing (how the brainstem generates rhythmic breaths and how we can simulate that), the mechanical aspects of lung inflation (and how to model volume changes and reflex feedback), and the integration of heart rate to provide biofeedback and enrich the user experience. The use of microphone and PPG data ensures the orb will be responsive to the user, truly “breathing with” them in synchrony. This design is ambitious in scope – essentially creating a simplified digital twin of the human respiratory control system – but it is feasible given modern computing capabilities and existing research models to borrow from.

The next step for the design team is to translate these research insights into a concrete design and implementation strategy. That likely means defining the exact equations and parameters for the breathing model, selecting libraries or writing code for real-time sensor processing, and designing the visual/tactile feedback that the orb will provide to the user. The design agent can use this research to ensure that every aspect of Brorb’s behavior has a basis in reality: e.g., the timing of the orb’s expansion should follow the kind of waveform a real diaphragm would produce, the color changes should correspond to meaningful physiological changes, etc. By grounding the design in neurophysiology and biomechanics, we increase the chances that interacting with Brorb will have genuine beneficial effects (like entraining the user’s breathing, enhancing their vagal tone, and reducing stress). This fulfills the mandate of going beyond a gimmicky animation to create something “mechanistically valid” and effective.

With a detailed model in hand and no hard constraints limiting us, Brorb can be an innovative tool that merges science and art – an orb that not only visualizes breathing but also embodies the complex beauty of the human body’s respiratory and autonomic regulation. The research presented here can now be handed off to the design and development team to guide the implementation of Brorb’s features, ensuring that the final product is both intellectually rigorous and deeply engaging for users.
